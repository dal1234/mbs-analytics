1. Purpose and scope

The model generates Monte Carlo paths of interest rates that drive a forecast of interest income on a portfolio of Fannie Mae fixed-rate MBS. The paths feed two things: the prepayment model, through the 30-year mortgage rate, and the reinvestment of returned principal, through 1-month SOFR. The output is a distribution of interest income over horizons of one to five years, from which Earnings at Risk (EaR) is read.

ItemDefinition
Intended useEaR on MBS interest income: the 5th percentile of income (95% confidence) and its dollar and percentage shortfall against the baseline forecast, for years 1 to 5, cumulative and annual.
Rates produced1-month SOFR, 10-year Treasury, 30-year fixed mortgage rate; optionally 1-, 2-, 5- and 30-year Treasury yields.
Balance sheetStatic: existing securities run off, returned principal is reinvested at 1-month SOFR, there are no new purchases and no funding side.
UsersThe CFO and the finance team of the capital markets group.
Out of scopeValuation, option-adjusted spread, economic value of equity, hedging, and any use that requires arbitrage-free pricing (see section 3). The prepayment model is a separate model with its own documentation; the engine shipped in the code is a placeholder.

2. Summary

The model is a real-world, baseline-centered, two-factor Gaussian model calibrated to public history.

  • Each simulated rate equals a baseline path plus a zero-mean random deviation. The baseline is an input: a forward curve, a house forecast or a flat curve all run through the same generator.
  • Two correlated mean-reverting factors, the deviation of the short rate and of the 10-year rate, drive the curve. Each has momentum: a move tends to continue for a few months before mean reversion takes over. A third independent factor drives the primary/secondary mortgage spread.
  • Parameters are estimated so that the fan is as wide at each horizon (3 to 60 months) as rates have historically ended up from the forward curve over that horizon: the same quantity the fan represents when the baseline is the forward curve.
  • The official run uses 5,000 monthly paths over 60 months with antithetic sampling and moment matching.
  • One calibration is official. Two alternatives are reported beside it as a model-risk range. Users do not set calibration parameters.

3. Theoretical framework

3.1 Two probability measures

Interest rate simulation serves two different purposes, and each requires its own probability measure [5, 13].

  • Risk-neutral measure (pricing). Absence of arbitrage implies that prices are expected discounted cash flows under a measure in which every asset earns the short rate. The drift of rates under this measure is determined by today's curve and the volatility structure [4]. It is not a forecast. Models in this class include Vasicek [1], Hull-White [2, 3] and the market models, and they are calibrated to option prices.
  • Real-world measure (forecasting and risk). Paths represent what rates may actually do. Dispersion is estimated from history, and the central path reflects a view of expected rates [7, 8].

The two measures share the same volatility and differ in drift by the market price of risk. EaR is a statement about the probability of an earnings outcome, so it is a real-world quantity. This model is therefore specified and calibrated under the real-world measure.

3.2 Centering on the baseline

The model does not forecast the level of rates. It takes the baseline as given and simulates deviations whose expected value is zero at every horizon. This has three consequences.

  • The EaR shortfall is measured against the same baseline that the income forecast is built on, so the two reconcile.
  • Changing the baseline does not change the model, its calibration or its validation.
  • When the baseline is the forward curve, the model treats forwards as expected future rates. Forward rates equal expected rates plus a term premium, and the premium is time-varying and not directly observable [9, 10]. Using forwards is a transparent, market-consistent convention, not a claim that forwards are unbiased forecasts. This is recorded as an assumption in section 12.

3.3 Why two Gaussian factors

Empirically, two to three factors explain almost all variation in the yield curve; the first two are interpreted as level and slope [6]. The income being modeled depends on two separate points on the curve: the long end, which sets the mortgage rate and therefore prepayments, and the short end, which sets the reinvestment rate. A one-factor model would force these to move in lockstep and would misstate the scenarios in which they diverge. Two factors is the minimum that represents this, and a third curve factor (curvature) has no exposure to act on in this portfolio.

The factors are taken to be observable rates, the short rate and the 10-year yield, in the spirit of yield-factor models [11]. Each follows a mean-reverting Gaussian process, the discrete-time form of the Vasicek process [1] extended with persistent shocks (section 4.1), and the pair has the same structure as the two-factor Gaussian (G2++ or two-factor Hull-White) model [3, 5]. If a valuation use is added later, the structure can be retained and recalibrated to option prices under the risk-neutral measure.

4. Model specification

Time is measured in months, t = 1, …, 60. All rates are decimals.

4.1 Factor dynamics

xS(t) = φS xS(t−1) + uS(t),   uS(t) = mS uS(t−1) + σS εS(t)     short-rate deviation
xL(t) = φL xL(t−1) + uL(t),   uL(t) = mL uL(t−1) + σL εL(t)     10-year deviation
s(t)  = φM s(t−1)  + σM εM(t)     mortgage spread deviation
x(0) = u(0) = s(0) = 0,   corr(εS, εL) = ρ,   εM independent,   ε ~ N(0,1)

φ is mean reversion, with a half-life of ln(0.5) / ln(φ) months. m is momentum: the shock itself persists, so a move tends to continue for a few months before reversion takes over, as the policy rate does in easing and tightening cycles [25, 26]. With m = 0 the factor is a first-order autoregression (model versions 1.0 and 1.1). With m ≠ 0 it is a second-order autoregression with real roots φ and m [27], which is still Gaussian and mean-reverting. Starting from zero, the standard deviation of a factor h months ahead is

sd[x(h)] = σ √( Σj=0..h−1 ψj2 ),    ψ0 = 1,   ψ1 = φ + m,   ψj = (φ + m) ψj−1 − φ m ψj−2

With m = 0 this reduces to σ √( (1 − φ2h) / (1 − φ2) ). σ sets how fast the fan opens, momentum how much faster it widens over the first one to two years than a square-root-of-time rule, and mean reversion how much wider it still gets between years 2 and 5. A first-order process has only the first and the last of these. It cannot produce a fan that widens fast through year 2 and then flattens, which is the shape of short-rate history (section 6.1).

4.2 Rates

rSOFR(t) = max( bSOFR(t) + xS(t), floor )
r10y(t)  = max( b10y(t) + xL(t), floor )
rmort(t) = max( bmort(t) + β xL(t) + s(t), floor )
rk(t)   = max( bk(t) + ak,S xS(t) + ak,L xL(t), floor )    other tenors k

b(t) is the baseline path for each rate. β is the sensitivity of the mortgage rate to the 10-year yield, and the loadings ak place other tenors between the two factors. Other tenors are provided for display and for prepayment models that use them; they carry no independent randomness.

4.3 The mortgage rate

The primary mortgage rate tracks the 10-year yield because the expected life of a 30-year mortgage is far shorter than its term [17]. The spread between the two is not constant. It moves with originator capacity, guarantee fees and volatility, and it is persistent [16]. The model represents it as a mean-reverting deviation around the baseline spread. The spread factor matters because the mortgage rate, not the Treasury yield, sets the refinancing incentive.

Data decisions

  • Short-rate proxy. SOFR begins in April 2018, which is too short to calibrate a five-year horizon. The effective federal funds rate is spliced in before that date. Both are overnight rates anchored by the same policy rate, so their changes over horizons of a quarter or longer are nearly identical.
  • Overnight versus 1-month SOFR. The reinvestment rate is 1-month SOFR, which is the market's expectation of the overnight rate over the coming month. Its level comes from the baseline. Its deviation is taken to equal the deviation of the overnight rate.
  • Sampling. Term yields and the mortgage rate use the last observation of the month, because changes in monthly averages have lower variance and spurious autocorrelation relative to point-in-time changes [15]. The overnight rate uses the monthly average to remove month-end and quarter-end funding noise; the policy rate is a step function, so averaging loses little.
  • Historical forwards. The calibration targets need, for every month of history, the forward baseline that a run on that date would have used. forwards.py bootstraps each month-end Treasury curve exactly as the run's baseline is built (curve.py) and records the implied forward 1-month rate and 10-year par yield 3 to 60 months ahead. Bill yields begin in 1982 (3-month) and 2001 (1-month); before that the short end of the curve is extrapolated from the 1-year point, which affects only the 1976 calibration. A month needs at least the 1-, 5- and 10-year yields.
  • Treasury, not swap, yields. Constant-maturity Treasury yields have a long, free, consistent history. Only their changes are used, so the level of the swap spread does not enter.

6. Calibration

Calibration is performed by calibrate() on a named historical window (section 13).

6.1 Factor volatility, momentum and mean reversion: horizon matching on forward deviations

The calibration target is the width of the simulated fan. The fan is a deviation from the baseline, and the official baseline is the forward curve, so the historical analogue of a simulated deviation is the deviation of the realized rate from the forward:

e(t, h) = r(t + h) − r(t) − [ f(t, h) − f(t, 0) ]

where f(t, h) is the rate implied for month t + h by month t's Treasury curve, built exactly as the run's forward baseline is built (section 5): the forward 1-month rate for the short rate and the forward 10-year par yield for the 10-year and the mortgage rate. For each factor, φ, m and σ are chosen so that the standard deviation of the simulated deviation h months ahead (section 4.1) matches the sample standard deviation of overlapping e(t, h), for h = 3, 12, 24, 36 and 60 months. The fit minimizes the weighted squared difference of the logarithms; the weight on each horizon is T/h, the approximate number of independent observations. φ is bounded to [0.80, 0.9995], m to [−0.5, 0.95], and a flag is raised if an estimate sits on a bound. In plain terms: the fan is as wide at each horizon as rates have historically ended up from the forward curve.

Why forward deviations. Versions 1.0 and 1.1 fitted the fan to raw h-month changes, because they treated public data as containing no forward history. Raw changes are the wrong target in both directions: they include moves the curve anticipated (a priced-in easing cycle is in the baseline, not the risk), and the forward also moves in ways a raw change does not show. On the official window the short rate's deviations from the forward are 5% to 10% narrower than its raw changes at every horizon (41 against 46 bp at 3 months, 135 against 149 at 12, 246 against 275 at 36). For the 10-year yield and the mortgage rate the two are within 4% of each other, because 10-year forwards are close to today's yield. The larger consequence of the change is the shape of the short-rate target, below.

Why momentum. The short rate's deviations from the forward widen quickly through year 2 and then level off (roughly 41, 135, 224, 246 and 240 bp at 3, 12, 24, 36 and 60 months on the official window). A first-order process cannot produce that shape: fitted to these targets its φ goes to the upper bound and it is still 19% too wide at 3 months and 28% to 39% too narrow at 12 to 36 months. Momentum is the one parameter that adds the missing shape. Policy rates move in runs because the central bank adjusts gradually [25, 26]; the momentum term is that gradualism. It is identified: on histories simulated from a first-order process of this length, the refitted momentum exceeds 0.38 in only 5% of cases, against an estimate near 0.78 for the short rate (V7 reports the range).

Why horizon matching. EaR depends on how far rates can move over one to five years. A model fitted only to month-to-month dynamics can match monthly volatility and still misstate multi-year dispersion, because monthly changes are autocorrelated (momentum) and because the least-squares estimate of mean reversion is biased toward reversion that is too fast in samples of this length [12, 22].

What the target leaves out. The standard deviation removes the mean deviation. Forwards have been biased forecasts: since 1990 the realized short rate ended on average 65 bp below its 12-month forward and more than 2 percentage points below its 5-year forward (the term premium, and three decades of falling rates) [9, 10, 28]. That bias is a property of the baseline, not of the fan, and is assumption A1. Using the root-mean-square deviation instead would add the historical bias to the width, which would double count it whenever the baseline is not the forward.

Challengers. The calibration diagnostics report a first-order process (m = 0) fitted to the same targets, the v1.1.0 calibration (first-order, fitted to raw changes) measured against the forward-deviation targets, and ordinary least squares with the Kendall bias correction [12]. A simulation study of the v1.1.0 first-order estimator on histories generated from known parameters (300 replications, 442 months, three parameter sets; estimator_study.py) gave the following for the 60-month fan width.

Fitted to deviations from an unbiased forecast, which is what the forward-deviation target is when forwards are unbiased, the production estimator recovers the generator's fan width: the V7 bootstrap, which measures each simulated history's deviations from its own conditional expectation, gives a mean ratio of about 0.98 at 12 months and 0.91 at 60 months. The shortfall at 60 months is the small-sample bias of long-horizon standard deviations from overlapping data. Every estimator carries sampling error of roughly 20% (one standard deviation) on five-year dispersion and about 10% at one year. That uncertainty cannot be reduced by running more paths, and it is the main reason alternative calibrations are reported (section 13).

6.2 Short/long correlation

ρ is solved so that the model's correlation of the short-rate and 10-year deviations 12 months ahead equals the historical correlation of their 12-month deviations from the forward. With momentum the two factors accumulate shocks with different weights, so the deviation correlation is smaller than the shock correlation:

corr[xS(12), xL(12)] = ρ Σ ψS,j ψL,j / √( Σ ψS,j2 Σ ψL,j2 ),   j = 0..11

and ρ is the historical correlation divided by the shape factor, capped at ±0.99. Historically the two are more highly correlated over a year than over a month, because the long end anticipates policy moves that the short rate delivers later. A model with a single correlation parameter cannot match every horizon, so the correlation is matched at the first reporting horizon; other horizons are recorded in the diagnostics. In the simulation study, the 12-month estimate was unbiased but about three times noisier than the monthly estimate (standard deviation 0.095 against 0.032); this is accepted as the cost of matching the relevant horizon.

6.3 Mortgage rate

The mortgage rate is calibrated the same way as the two factors: so that its fan is as wide at each horizon as the mortgage rate has historically ended up from its forward over that horizon. The forward mortgage rate is the forward 10-year yield plus today's spread, as in the run's baseline.

  • β is the regression coefficient of 12-month mortgage-rate deviations from the forward on 12-month 10-year deviations from the forward, the horizon at which the short/long correlation is matched (section 6.2). The pass-through of Treasury moves into the survey rate rises with the horizon: on the official window it is about 0.8 at 3 months, 0.9 at 12 months and slightly above 1 at 36 and 60 months (results below). Short horizons understate it because the survey rate lags the Treasury market and is published on a different day from the Treasury month-end.
  • With β and the 10-year factor fixed, φM and σM are chosen so that the standard deviation of the simulated mortgage-rate deviation h months ahead,
    sd[dmort(h)] = √( β2 sd[xL(h)]2 + sd[s(h)]2 )
    matches the sample standard deviation of overlapping h-month mortgage-rate deviations from the forward, for the same horizons and weights as section 6.1. The spread therefore carries whatever mortgage-rate dispersion the 10-year factor does not.
  • The spread shock is independent of the rate shocks. See the limitation in section 12.

History. Version 1.0.0 took β from 3-month changes and fitted the spread by autoregression. The 10-year fan matched history, but β = 0.77 passed through too little of a multi-year move and the fast-reverting spread could not make it up: the mortgage fan was 20% to 24% narrower than history at three to five years. Because the mortgage rate drives refinancing, that understated the downside of income at the longer horizons. Version 1.1.0 fixed this on raw changes; version 1.2.0 applies the same method to forward deviations. The v1.1.0 β is kept as a challenger in the calibration diagnostics.

7. Simulation

Implemented in simulate_paths() with settings held in SimConfig.

  • Time step and horizon. Monthly steps for 60 months, matching the cash-flow frequency of MBS and the input frequency of prepayment models. The autoregressive recursion is exact at a monthly step; there is no discretization error.
  • Initial condition. Deviations start at zero: today's rates are known and equal the baseline's starting point.
  • Antithetic sampling. Half the paths are drawn and the other half are their mirror images. The sample mean of every deviation is then exactly zero, and estimates of statistics that are close to linear in rates become more precise [13].
  • Moment matching. At each month, shocks are rescaled across paths to unit sample variance, so the simulated dispersion matches the calibrated dispersion and does not vary with the random seed [13].
  • Seed. Fixed and recorded, so every reported figure can be reproduced exactly.
  • Floor. Every simulated rate is floored at 0%. A Gaussian model permits negative rates; U.S. policy and Treasury rates have not been negative, and the floor follows the view that the nominal rate behaves like an option with a zero strike [19]. The floor truncates the falling-rate tail and raises the mean of floored paths above the baseline. Both effects are measured in validation tests V1 and V4.
  • Alternative sampler. Scrambled Sobol sequences are available as an option and are used as an independent check on the pseudo-random results.

Number of paths

The estimate of a percentile has sampling error. If n independent paths are used to estimate the quantile at probability p, the probability level actually captured has standard error √( p(1 − p) / n ) [13]. For p = 5%:

The official run uses 5,000 paths, which resolves both the 5th and the 1st percentile, so the 95% and 99% measures and expected shortfall come from one path set. A 20,000-path run is produced at each calibration to demonstrate convergence (test V5). 300 paths is acceptable for means and standard deviations and is not used for reported percentiles. Each reported percentile carries a 95% bootstrap confidence interval [14] in which antithetic pairs are resampled together.

Why 5,000 (revised from 1,000). With 1,000 paths the sample correlation between shocks that are independent by design is typically around ±0.1. Moment matching fixes each shock's variance but not those correlations. The mortgage rate combines the 10-year factor and the spread, so its simulated dispersion missed the calibrated width by more than 5% at some horizon for about 40% of random seeds, and the 1,000-path five-year 5th percentile fell outside the 5,000-path interval. At 5,000 paths both errors are within about 1%.

8. Earnings-at-Risk measurement

Implemented in ear_summary(). Income on each path is supplied by the income engine, which applies the prepayment model to the mortgage rate path and reinvests returned principal at the 1-month SOFR path.

Income at confidence c  = (1 − c) quantile of simulated income    (5th percentile for c = 95%)
Shortfall ($)  = baseline income − income at confidence
Shortfall (%)  = shortfall ($) / baseline income
  • Baseline income is income on the baseline rate path, run through the same engine.
  • Horizons. The statistics are computed for years 1 to 5, both cumulative (months 1 to 12k) and annual (months 12(k−1)+1 to 12k). The percentile is taken separately for each horizon, because the paths that are worst in year 1 are not the paths that are worst over five years.
  • The percentile is taken on income, not on rates. No assumption is made about which direction of rates is adverse. For a security whose coupon is above SOFR, a prepayment replaces a higher-yielding asset with a lower-yielding one, so falling rates reduce income twice: more principal is returned and it earns less. For a security whose coupon is below SOFR, a prepayment raises income, and the adverse outcomes are driven mainly by the SOFR path. A portfolio contains both, and the sign of the exposure can change as rates move.
  • Mean versus baseline. Mean simulated income differs from baseline income because income is a nonlinear function of rates. The difference is reported (mean_minus_baseline_pct) and is a measure of the convexity of income, not an error.
  • Income definition. The reference engine measures coupon interest plus reinvestment interest. If reported income includes amortization of purchase premium and discount, the production engine must include it; the rate paths and the EaR calculation are unaffected.

With momentum the short rate's 1-year fan is wider than the 10-year's, so on an upward-sloping baseline the simulated curve inverts more often than it has historically (about 31% of path-months against 18% at the 2026-09 calibration): disclosed, not adjusted.
V4Floor incidenceShare of path-months at the floor, and share of paths that touch it, by rate. Reported. If more than 5% of paths touch the floor, EaR is rerun with the floor at −0.50% and the difference disclosed.
V5ConvergenceEaR at 1,000, 5,000 and 20,000 paths with bootstrap interval half-widths. 20,000-path result inside the 95% confidence interval of the official 5,000-path result. (Revised from "tested result inside the benchmark interval", which ignores the tested run's own sampling error and fails about a third of the time on converged results.)
V6Benchmark shocksIncome under parallel shifts of ±100 and ±200 basis points, located as a percentile of the simulated income distribution. Direction and ordering consistent with the simulated distribution. See note below.
V7Estimation errorParametric bootstrap: histories are simulated from the fitted model (with momentum), their deviations measured from their own conditional expectation, refitted with the production estimator, and the spread of implied 12- and 60-month dispersion and of the momentum estimate reported. Reported with the EaR as a statement of calibration uncertainty.
V8Calibration sensitivityEaR under each named calibration (calibration_sensitivity()). Range reported beside the official figure.

Note on V6. An instantaneous parallel shock applies in full from the first month. Simulated paths start at the baseline and diffuse away from it, so an immediate 100-basis-point shift is a far more extreme event over the first year than the same shift reached at month 12. Instantaneous shocks will therefore sit deep in the tail at the one-year horizon. This is expected and is a reason to present the two measures as answering different questions.

Ongoing monitoring

  • Coverage backtest. Each month, record the 5th and 95th percentile bands produced 12 months earlier and whether the realized deviation from that baseline fell inside. Over time the exceedance frequency is tested against the nominal 10% [24]. Overlapping 12-month windows are not independent, so the test is indicative until several years of results exist.
  • Stability. Track each estimated parameter across calibrations. A change of more than 20% in 12- or 60-month dispersion between consecutive calibrations triggers review.
  • Challenger gap. Track the difference between production and challenger dispersion (section 6.1).

12. Assumptions and limitations

#Assumption or limitationEffect and mitigation
A1The baseline is the expected path of rates.A forward-curve baseline includes a term premium [9, 10]: since 1990 the realized short rate ended on average 65 bp below its 12-month forward (section 6.1). The fan width excludes this bias by construction. The shortfall is measured against the baseline and is insensitive to modest errors in its level; the level of income is not. Alternative baselines can be run without changing the model.
A2Volatility is constant over the horizon.Rate volatility clusters. The model reports average dispersion and will understate risk at the start of a volatile regime. Mitigated by the high-volatility calibration (V8).
A3Volatility does not depend on the level of rates.Evidence indicates that it rises with the level [18]. Material only if the baseline is far from the levels in the calibration window.
A4Shocks are Gaussian.No jumps or fat tails in monthly shocks. Multi-year sums of shocks are close to Gaussian in any case, and the 95% level is less sensitive to tail shape than 99% would be.
A5One correlation between short and long rates.Cannot reproduce the full lead-lag pattern between the long end and the policy rate. Correlation is matched at 12 months; other horizons are disclosed.
A6Mean reversion is poorly identified.Five-year dispersion carries estimation error of roughly 20% (V7), and the long-horizon target is biased low by a few percent in samples of this length. This is the largest quantifiable source of model uncertainty and is disclosed with the EaR. The 10-year factor's momentum is not well identified (V7 range); the short rate's is.
A6bHistorical forward deviations represent the risk around today's forward baseline.The fan is fitted to how far rates ended up from the forward in the calibration window (section 6.1). The historical forwards are bootstrapped from the Treasury curve by the same method as the run's baseline; before 1982 the short end is extrapolated (1976 calibration only). With a flat or house-view baseline the same fan is used, which assumes those baselines miss by as much as forwards did. Version 1.1.0 fitted raw changes instead; the challenger in the diagnostics shows the difference.
A7Rates are floored at zero.Truncates the falling-rate tail, which is adverse for premium-coupon holdings. Incidence measured in V4 with a rerun trigger.
A8The mortgage spread is independent of rates.The spread has tended to widen when rates fall quickly and originators are capacity-constrained [16], which delays the pass-through to refinancing. Independence therefore tends to overstate prepayment response in sharp rallies, which is conservative for premium-coupon holdings.
A9The 1-month SOFR deviation equals the overnight-rate deviation.Ignores variation in the one-month term spread, which is small relative to the short-rate dispersion over 12 months or more.
A10Other tenors are exact combinations of the two factors.Understates their idiosyncratic variation. Immaterial unless the prepayment model is driven by those tenors.
A11The model is not arbitrage-free.Acceptable for income simulation under the real-world measure. The paths must not be used for discounting or pricing.
A12History before April 2018 uses the federal funds rate for SOFR.Basis between the two is small relative to rate changes over a quarter or more.
A13Static balance sheet; rates are the only risk factor.EaR excludes purchase volume, spread risk on new investments and prepayment model error. Prepayment model risk is governed separately.

13.2 Why users do not set calibration parameters

  • Each window or exclusion is a different calibration of the model. Supervisory guidance expects a model to be validated in the configuration in which it is used [20, 21]; an open toggle creates configurations that have not been.
  • A reported risk measure must be reproducible and comparable from period to period. Changes in EaR should come from the portfolio and the market, not from settings.
  • The choice of window is a modeling judgment that belongs to the model owner and is documented here.

Sensitivity to calibration is still shown: the three named calibrations appear side by side (V8). Users may change inputs and views, which alter the question and not the model: the baseline, the horizon, cumulative or annual income, the portfolio segment, deterministic shock overlays and the percentile bands on the fan chart.

13.3 Change control

  • Recalibration. Annually, and off-cycle if a monitoring trigger in section 11 is breached.
  • Run record. Every run stores the model version, calibration name, window dates, parameter values, seed, path count and baseline identifier.
  • Material changes. A change to the factor structure, distribution, estimator, official window or floor is a model change requiring revalidation. A scheduled recalibration on the existing window definition is not.
